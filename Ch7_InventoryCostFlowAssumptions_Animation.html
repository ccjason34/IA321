The Scenario: Three Purchases in May, One Total Sale

Boc Apparel buys the same shirt 3 times during May — each time the supplier's price goes up. Boc also makes two sales: 6 units on May 10 and 12 units on May 28 (18 total). What is the cost of those 18 shirts? The answer depends on the cost flow method and the inventory system. Same purchases. Same sales. Different answers.

🎯 The Question

How do we split $360 between COGS and ending inventory?

We have $360 of goods available and we sold 18 of 30 units. Each method gives a different answer about which $360 became COGS and which became ending inventory.

The math always works out so that COGS + Ending Inventory = $360. But where each method draws the line is different — and that single decision shifts net income, taxes, and inventory on the balance sheet.
Three Methods, Three Rules
Each method follows a single, simple rule for which units left the shelf when a sale happened.
FIFO
First-In, First-Out
The oldest units sell first. Newer units stay in inventory.
Physical analogy: a milk carton — old in front, new in back.
LIFO
Last-In, First-Out
The newest units sell first. Older units stay in inventory.
Physical analogy: a stack of plates — top plate goes first.
Weighted Average
Blend Everything
Treat every unit as if it cost the average. No "old" or "new."
Physical analogy: pour all batches into one bin, mix.
Why care? When prices rise (like this scenario), the three methods produce different COGS, ending inventory, gross profit, net income, and income tax. Same physical units, same money — but the financial statements look different.
Bottom line: The cost flow method is an accounting assumption — not a description of which physical shirt was actually sold. Boc's customers can't tell the difference. The accountants can.

Periodic vs Perpetual — Same Purchases, Sales Split in Time
Earlier, the animation used periodic: one big computation at month-end. Now let's see what changes if there's an intermediate sale on May 10 — a perpetual system updates COGS at every transaction. FIFO stays the same. LIFO changes because it can only use layers that exist on each sale date.
Perpetual = record COGS every time a sale happens. Sales happen on May 10 and May 28, so under perpetual we do not wait until May 31. Each sale immediately removes units from inventory. Cause & effect: Perpetual LIFO substitutes 6 cheaper units (@ $10) for 6 newer ones (@ $12) on the May 10 sale. That's 6 × ($12 − $10) = $12 less in COGS. The same $12 stays in ending inventory ($136 vs $124).
Why LIFO changes, but FIFO does not
Periodic waits until month-end; perpetual records each sale when it happens. That timing changes which LIFO layer is available.

May 10: 6 @ $10 = $60

The $12 and $14 layers did not exist on May 10, so perpetual LIFO could not use them for that sale. FIFO still matches because "oldest first" is oldest first whether you compute it now or at month-end.
What if the May 10 sale moved to a later date?
Under perpetual, sale timing decides whether LIFO matches periodic. Move that one sale just a few days later — past the May 15 purchase — and divergence disappears. FIFO is unaffected throughout.

The trigger: LIFO perpetual differs from periodic only when a sale happens before a layer that the month-end periodic calculation can include. Push the May 10 sale just one day past May 15, and the $12 layer is suddenly available — the divergence vanishes.
The pattern to remember:
  • FIFO — periodic and perpetual match: oldest costs leave first either way.
  • LIFO — watch the sale date. Perpetual LIFO can only use layers that have already been purchased on that date.
Bottom line: The cost flow method and the inventory system are two separate choices. For FIFO, periodic and perpetual match. For LIFO, the same purchases and same total sale can produce different COGS depending on whether you record continuously or wait until month-end.
Why the Choice Matters — Rising Prices
Suppose Boc sold those 18 shirts (6 on May 10 + 12 on May 28) for $25 each. Revenue is the same in all three columns: $450. But COGS isn't — so gross profit, net income, and ending inventory all shift.

The key pattern when prices rise: FIFO produces the highest net income (uses old, cheap costs in COGS) but also the highest tax bill. LIFO produces the lowest net income — and the lowest tax bill. Weighted Average sits in the middle. When prices fall, the pattern reverses.
Why companies choose LIFO: Lower reported income → lower current taxes (real cash savings). U.S. GAAP allows LIFO; IFRS does not. The LIFO reserve is the dollar gap between LIFO inventory and what FIFO inventory would have been — here, $164 − $124 = $40.
