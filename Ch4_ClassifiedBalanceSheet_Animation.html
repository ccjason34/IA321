Why Classify the Balance Sheet?
A simple list of all assets and liabilities mixed together is hard to interpret. A classified balance sheet groups items into meaningful categories so users can quickly answer questions like: Can the company pay its bills next year? How much of its financing is short-term?
From the textbook: "A classified balance sheet groups together similar assets and similar liabilities, using a number of standard classifications and sections. This is useful because items within a group have similar economic characteristics."
⏱️ The One-Year Rule (or Operating Cycle, whichever is longer)
Current = expected to be converted to cash, sold, or consumed within one year (or the operating cycle if longer). Non-current (long-term) = everything else. This single rule splits both the asset side and the liability side of the balance sheet.
The five major sections
① Current Assets
Will be converted to cash or used up within 1 year. Listed in order of liquidity:
  • Cash & Cash Equivalents
  • Short-term Investments
  • Accounts Receivable
  • Inventory
  • Prepaid Expenses
② Non-Current Assets
Held for more than 1 year. Includes:
  • Long-term Investments — stocks, bonds held > 1 year
  • Property, Plant & Equipment — land, buildings, equipment (less accum. depreciation)
  • Intangible Assets — goodwill, patents, trademarks
③ Current Liabilities
Due within 1 year. Includes:
  • Accounts Payable
  • Notes Payable (short-term)
  • Salaries & Wages Payable
  • Interest Payable
  • Unearned Revenue
  • Current portion of long-term debt
④ Long-Term Liabilities
Due in more than 1 year:
  • Bonds Payable
  • Long-term Notes Payable
  • Lease Liabilities
  • Deferred Tax Liabilities
  • Pension Obligations
⑤ Stockholders' Equity
Owners' claim on the company:
  • Capital Stock — Common Stock, Preferred Stock
  • Additional Paid-in Capital — amounts paid above par
  • Retained Earnings — accumulated profits not yet distributed
  • Treasury Stock (contra) — shares the company has repurchased

Why the Distinction Matters — Liquidity & Solvency
The classification isn't just bookkeeping. It powers the most-watched financial ratios. Here's how Boc Apparel's classified balance sheet drives four key analytical measures.
💰 Working Capital
Current Assets − Current Liabilities
$155,000 − $70,000
$85,000
The cushion of liquid resources after paying off short-term debts. Positive working capital means Boc has enough current assets to meet its short-term obligations and still operate.
📊 Current Ratio
Current Assets ÷ Current Liabilities
$155,000 ÷ $70,000
2.21 : 1
Boc has $2.21 of current assets for every $1 of current liabilities. A ratio above 2.0 is generally considered healthy — the company can comfortably pay short-term debts as they come due.
⚡ Quick Ratio (Acid Test)
(Cash + ST Inv. + A/R) ÷ Current Liab.
($35,000 + $15,000 + $42,000) ÷ $70,000
1.31 : 1
Excludes Inventory and Prepaid Expenses (slower to convert to cash). A ratio above 1.0 means Boc can cover all current liabilities without needing to sell inventory.
⚖️ Debt to Total Assets
Total Liabilities ÷ Total Assets
$270,000 ÷ $540,000
50%
Half of Boc's assets are financed by creditors; the other half by stockholders. A measure of long-term solvency — lower percentages indicate less reliance on borrowed funds.
The big takeaway: Without classification, none of these ratios could be calculated. Knowing only "Total Assets = $540,000" tells you nothing about whether the company can pay its bills next month. The classification turns a list of numbers into a story about liquidity (can they pay short-term?) and solvency (can they pay long-term?).
