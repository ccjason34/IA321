The Scenario: A Fire at Boc Apparel

On the night of June 30, 2022, a fire destroyed the warehouse at Boc Apparel. The inventory is gone. The accounting team has the books showing beginning inventory ($25,000), net purchases for the year ($80,000), and net sales ($100,000) — but no physical count of what was on the shelves when the fire started. The insurance company needs an estimate of the loss this week. How do we estimate inventory we cannot count?

The Gross Profit Method — In Three Steps
Work backward from sales. If we know the typical relationship between sales and cost (the gross profit %), we can estimate the cost of what was sold — and what's left over must be the inventory.
① Goods Available
Add up everything that could have been sold during the period.

② Estimate COGS

③ Ending Inventory
What was available but not sold must still be in inventory (or destroyed in the fire).
Goods Available − Estimated COGS
Bottom line: The gross profit method is just Goods Available − Estimated COGS. The only trick is computing COGS using the cost ratio, not the gross profit rate.

Common mistake to avoid: Don't multiply sales by 30% (the GP rate) — that gives you estimated gross profit, not COGS. Always use the cost ratio (70%) to estimate COGS.
When the Problem Says "25% Markup on Cost"
Sometimes you're not given the gross profit rate directly. Instead, the problem says "the company applies a 25% markup on cost." Don't panic — the gross profit method still works. You just need to convert that markup into a cost ratio first.
$100 cost + 25% markup → see where 80% comes from
Cost = $100
80% of selling price

20%
Selling Price = $100 + $25 = $125 (this is 100%)
Cost ratio = $100 ÷ $125 = 80%
Then: Estimated COGS = Sales × 80%  ·  Same gross profit method, just a different rate.

Compare to GP % of Sales: With a 70% cost ratio (30% GP of sales), ending inventory was $35,000. With an 80% cost ratio (25% markup on cost), ending inventory is $25,000. Different rates → different estimates. The structure of the calculation is identical; only the rate changes.
25% Markup on Cost ≠ 25% Gross Profit of Sales
They describe the same $25 of profit — but they use different denominators. Markup-on-cost divides by cost ($100); gross-profit-of-sales divides by sales ($125). One picture explains it all.

The shortcut formula: If the problem gives you a markup-on-cost rate m, then:
  • GP % of sales = m / (1 + m)   e.g., 25% / 125% = 20%
  • Cost ratio = 1 / (1 + m)   e.g., 100% / 125% = 80%
