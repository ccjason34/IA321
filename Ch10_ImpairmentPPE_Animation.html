Impairment Uses Two Different Comparisons
For long-lived assets held and used under U.S. GAAP, impairment is a two-step process. Step 1 asks whether the asset is recoverable. Step 2 measures the loss.
📌 Memory cue: undiscounted cash flows decide WHETHER there is a loss; fair value decides HOW MUCH the loss is.
Core rule: first compare carrying amount with undiscounted future cash flows. If carrying amount is not recoverable, measure the impairment loss using fair value.
⚠
Student trap: do not use fair value in Step 1. Step 1 is the recoverability gate — it uses undiscounted cash flows.
The two different comparisons (this is the heart of the lesson)
STEP 1 · Recoverability gate
Compare:Carrying amount vs. Undiscounted CF
Purpose:Should we impair? (yes/no)
When used:Always — the screen
Pass rule:CA ≤ undisc. CF → no impairment
STEP 2 · Measurement
Compare:Carrying amount vs. Fair value
Purpose:How much is the loss?
When used:Only after Step 1 FAILS
Loss formula:CA − FV = loss
Why undiscounted in Step 1 but fair value in Step 2? Step 1 is a forgiving screen — if the asset can't recover even at the inflated undiscounted amount, you've definitely got a problem. Step 2 is the real economic measurement, so it uses fair value (which reflects discounting and current market reality).

🚫
No reversal under U.S. GAAP. Once an asset (held and used) is written down for impairment, the new lower carrying amount becomes the cost basis. Subsequent recoveries in fair value are not recognized — even if the asset's value bounces back later. (IFRS allows reversal in some cases — but not GAAP.)
