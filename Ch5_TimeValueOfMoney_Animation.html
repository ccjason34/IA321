Money Has a Time Value
A dollar today is not the same as a dollar a year from now. If you have a dollar today, you can invest it and earn interest. If you have a promise of a dollar in the future, you have to wait — and waiting has a cost. Two operations connect the present to the future: compounding moves money forward in time; discounting moves it backward.
The core insight: The interest rate (also called the discount rate) is the bridge between dollars today and dollars in the future. The same formula handles both directions — one direction multiplies, the other divides.
📈 Future Value (FV)
What it answers: If I invest $X today at rate r for n years, how much will I have?
FV = PV × (1 + r)n
The operation: Compounding — multiply by (1 + r) for each year forward. Each period's interest itself earns interest in subsequent periods. This is why compound interest is so powerful.
📉 Present Value (PV)
What it answers: If I'm promised $Y in n years at discount rate r, what is that worth today?
PV = FV ÷ (1 + r)n
The operation: Discounting — divide by (1 + r) for each year backward. A future amount is always worth less today, because today's dollar could be invested.
Why this matters in accounting: Time value of money underlies almost every long-term measurement in financial reporting — bond prices, lease liabilities, pension obligations, impairment tests, capital budgeting decisions, and notes receivable/payable. If you understand PV/FV, you have the foundation for most of intermediate accounting.
