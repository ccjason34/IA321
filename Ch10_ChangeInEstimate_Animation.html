A Change in Estimate Is Prospective
When useful life, residual value, or both are revised, depreciation is not recomputed for prior years. The company uses the asset's current book value and spreads the remaining depreciable amount over the revised remaining life.
📌 Memory cue: change in estimate means "from here forward."
Core rule: no restatement, no catch-up entry, no correction of prior depreciation.
⚠
Student trap: do not go back to Year 1 and recalculate old depreciation. Prior years stay as originally recorded.
What "prospective" actually means — wrong path vs right path
❌ WRONG (retrospective)
Recompute 2025 & 2026 at the new $13K rate.
Restate prior financial statements.
Adjust retained earnings.
This is what you'd do for an error correction or a change in principle — but NOT for a change in estimate.
✓ RIGHT (prospective)
Leave 2025 & 2026 at $14K each.
Apply $13K starting in 2027.
Prior statements stay as issued.
No catch-up entry. No restatement. The new estimate just changes future depreciation.
1. Keep Prior Years
Depreciation already recorded stays in the books.
Old depreciation remains old depreciation
2. Start With Book Value
At the revision date, find current book value.
Cost - accumulated depreciation
3. Recompute Going Forward
Use revised residual value and revised remaining useful life.
(Book value - new residual) / remaining life

Key point: the new $13,000 annual depreciation starts in 2027. The 2025 and 2026 amounts stay $14,000.

Why Debit or Credit?
Estimate rule
No catch-up entry. Use the new amount prospectively.
Expense rule
Expenses increase with debits.
Contra-asset rule
Accumulated depreciation increases with credits.
Cash check
No cash is paid when depreciation is recorded.
